How venture capital actually works.

The primer I wish someone had handed me: fund mechanics, the math, the term sheet, and the vocabulary — written in plain English by an MBA candidate living inside this material at USC Marshall, with ten-plus years of investing behind it.

The short version

VC is equity money for companies banks won't touch.

A startup with no profits, no collateral, and a decent chance of dying can't borrow. So instead it sells ownership — shares — to investors who accept that most of these bets fail, because the ones that work can return 100x. That's venture capital: high-risk equity financing, in exchange for a piece of the upside.

Everything else on this page — funds, term sheets, SAFEs, dilution — is machinery built around that one trade. Understand the trade, and the machinery starts making sense.

The players

Who's actually in the room.

LPs — Limited Partners
The money behind the money: pension funds, university endowments, family offices, wealthy individuals. They commit capital to a fund and stay hands-off.
GPs — General Partners
The VCs themselves. They raise the fund from LPs, pick the startups, sit on boards, and are legally on the hook for running it all.
The fund
A pool of committed capital — say $100M — with a roughly 10-year life. New investments happen in the first 3–5 years; the rest is reserved for follow-ons and waiting for exits.
2 and 20
How GPs get paid: about 2% of the fund per year as a management fee (keeps the lights on), plus 20% of the profits — called carry — once LPs get their money back.
Founders
Sell slices of their company round by round. Their job in this system: make the slice everyone holds worth vastly more than the whole was at the start.
The fund math

The power law runs everything.

Venture returns don't follow a bell curve. In a typical portfolio, a large share of companies return less than the money invested, a handful do fine, and one or two outliers generate nearly all of the fund's returns. This is the power law, and it explains almost every VC behavior that looks strange from the outside.

It's why investors ask "can this be huge?" before "is this safe?" — a startup that safely triples doesn't move a fund. It's why VCs push for big markets, fast growth, and outcomes that can return the entire fund from a single position. And it's why they target meaningful ownership — often 10–20% at entry — because owning 1% of even a great outcome isn't enough to matter.

Why "return the fund" is the bar

A $100M fund owns 15% of a startup that exits for $700M. Its stake is worth $105M — one deal just returned the whole fund. Everything else in the portfolio is now upside. That's the outcome every partner is underwriting for.

Raising rounds

Rounds, valuations, and dilution.

Startups raise in stages, each round funding the next 18–24 months of proof. The labels are conventions, not laws, but roughly:

Pre-seed
Idea and team. Money from angels and pre-seed funds, often on SAFEs, to build a first version.
Seed
Early signs of life — users, pilots, first revenue. Institutional seed funds join; the goal is proving product-market fit.
Series A
A repeatable engine. A lead VC prices the round, takes a board seat, and funds scaling what already works.
Series B and beyond
Pouring fuel: growth capital to expand a proven model, with later rounds looking more and more like private equity.

Worked example — pre-money, post-money, dilution

A VC invests $2M at an $8M pre-money valuation. Post-money = 8 + 2 = $10M. The investor's stake = 2 ÷ 10 = 20%. Every existing holder — founders included — just got diluted by 20%: a founder who owned 60% now owns 48%. Dilution isn't failure; it's the price of fuel. The question is always whether 48% of the bigger thing beats 60% of the smaller one.

The term sheet

The six terms that actually matter.

A term sheet is the non-binding blueprint of a deal. Valuation gets the headlines, but these clauses decide who gets what when things go right — and especially when they don't:

Liquidation preference
Investors get their money out first in a sale. The standard is 1x non-participating: take your money back, or convert to common and take your ownership share — whichever is worth more, not both.
Participating preferred
The aggressive version: money back and a share of what's left — the "double dip." Founders resist it; in decent markets it's rare.
Anti-dilution
Protects investors if a later round prices lower (a down round). Broad-based weighted average is the fair standard; full ratchet — repricing as if they'd invested at the new low price — is punitive.
Pro-rata rights
The right to invest again in future rounds to maintain your ownership percentage. How early investors ride winners all the way up.
Option pool shuffle
Investors often require an employee option pool created before the round — carved out of the founders' side. A 10% pool inside an $8M pre effectively lowers what founders are really being paid for their shares. Always ask: pool pre or post?
Board & protective provisions
Who controls decisions: board seats, plus investor veto rights over big moves (selling the company, raising again, changing the charter). Control terms outlast valuation terms.
SAFEs & notes

How early money skips the valuation fight.

Pricing a company that's two people and a demo is guesswork, so early rounds often defer it. The instrument of choice is the SAFE — Simple Agreement for Future Equity — money now that converts into shares at the next priced round.

Valuation cap
The maximum valuation your money converts at. Invest $100K at a $5M cap and the company raises later at $20M — you convert as if it were worth $5M, getting roughly 4x the shares of new investors.
Discount
The alternative sweetener: convert at, say, 20% below the next round's price. When a SAFE has both, the investor takes whichever converts better.
Post-money SAFE
The current YC standard. Ownership is knowable immediately: $500K on a $5M post-money cap = 10% (before the next round dilutes everyone). Cleaner math, slightly more founder dilution.
Convertible note
The SAFE's older sibling — legally debt, so it adds an interest rate (2–8%) and a maturity date. Same conversion idea, more paperwork and a ticking clock.
The VC method

How a VC prices a startup in 60 seconds.

Forget DCFs — there are no cash flows to discount. The VC method works backwards from the exit:

Worked example — backing into a valuation

A fund considers a $5M check and, given the risk, needs a 10x — so the stake must be worth $50M at exit. They believe the company can exit at $500M, so they need 10% ownership at exit. But future rounds will dilute them — assume they'll keep only 75% of today's stake. So they need 10% ÷ 0.75 ≈ 13.3% today. For $5M to buy 13.3%, post-money must be 5 ÷ 0.133 ≈ $37.5M. That's the offer — and now you know exactly which assumptions to argue with.

Every input is debatable — exit value, target multiple, dilution — which is precisely the point. The method doesn't remove judgment; it shows you where the judgment lives.

Glossary sprint

Twelve terms that unlock the rest.

Cap table
The spreadsheet of truth: who owns what, at which price, with which rights.
Dilution
Your percentage shrinking as new shares are issued. Normal; the goal is a smaller slice of a much bigger pie.
Runway
Months of cash left at the current burn rate. Fundraising starts long before it hits zero.
Burn rate
Net cash a startup spends per month.
Lead investor
The VC who sets the round's terms, writes the biggest check, and usually takes the board seat.
Bridge round
A smaller raise to extend runway to a milestone — a bridge to somewhere, ideally.
Down round
Raising at a lower valuation than last time. Triggers anti-dilution, bruises morale, beats dying.
Dry powder
Committed capital a fund hasn't deployed yet.
MOIC
Multiple on invested capital — total value ÷ money in. The simplest "how did we do."
TVPI / DPI
Fund-level MOIC: TVPI counts paper value plus cash returned; DPI counts only cash actually returned to LPs. DPI is the one you can spend.
IRR
Annualized return that accounts for timing — because getting 3x in four years beats 3x in twelve.
Carry
The GP's 20% share of fund profits. The reason the job pays.
